# Bundling

**In one sentence:** Selling many products together for one price, like a streaming tier that carries another company's shows. ([[2026-08-25-Netflix-to-Sell-Streaming-Services-Streamers-as-Aggregators-Revisiting-Roku|Streamers as Aggregators]])

## Why it matters
In pay TV, Thompson noted in 2015 that fees paid per subscriber were more profitable and steady than ads, so must-see shows and sports mattered most; sports fans are why ESPN earned the most (2015 source). ([[2015-07-08-Netflix-and-the-Conservation-of-Attractive-Profits|Netflix and Attractive Profits]]) He now compares enterprise software to cable TV: stickier than theory says, but with long-term risk to terminal value (what a business is worth far into the future). ([[2026-05-14-Interview-Ben-Thompson-MoffettNathanson|MoffettNathanson interview]]) Bundles can fail: Stratechery judges Xbox Game Pass, its subscription bundle, a failure. ([[2026-07-10-XBOX-On-the-Rocks|XBOX On the Rocks]])

## Worked example: YouTube and Peacock, July 2026
YouTube signed a five-year deal to bring all of Peacock's shows into YouTube Premium, its $16 a month ad-free tier. ([[2026-08-25-Netflix-to-Sell-Streaming-Services-Streamers-as-Aggregators-Revisiting-Roku|Streamers as Aggregators]]) Thompson argues YouTube is bundling Peacock rather than selling it separately. ([[2026-08-25-Netflix-to-Sell-Streaming-Services-Streamers-as-Aggregators-Revisiting-Roku|Streamers as Aggregators]]) He says YouTube is free to experiment because Premium is not its core business, just as Prime Video was not Amazon's. ([[2026-08-25-Netflix-to-Sell-Streaming-Services-Streamers-as-Aggregators-Revisiting-Roku|Streamers as Aggregators]]) Amazon plays the same game on a bigger stage: it pays $1 billion a year for Thursday Night Football, bundled into Prime (2021 source). ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon|Gallaugher, Amazon]], p.278)
