# Vertical Integration

**In one sentence:** Vertical integration means a company owns more of the steps that make and deliver its product, like Zara, which makes nearly 60 percent of its clothes in-house while H&M uses 900 suppliers and owns no factories. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch02-Zara|Gallaugher, Zara, p.79]])

## Why it matters
Owning the steps buys speed: Zara's boss said the plan was to learn what customers want, then deliver it fast through its own factories and logistics. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch02-Zara|Gallaugher, Zara, p.78]]) Even with higher factory costs, Zara's parent earned gross margins (money left from each sale after the cost of making it) of nearly 60 percent, against 39 percent at Gap. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch02-Zara|Gallaugher, Zara, p.81]]) But the edge can fade: Dell's lead shrank once rivals' contract factories copied it. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch02-Zara|Gallaugher, Zara, p.87]])

## Worked example: Amazon shipping, 2013 to 2023
In December 2013 UPS could not handle Amazon's holiday rush, which cost Amazon millions in refunds. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon|Gallaugher, Amazon, p.260]]) As late as 2015, FedEx, UPS, and the Postal Service still carried more than 97 percent of Amazon's US packages. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon|Gallaugher, Amazon, p.259]]) So Amazon built its own fleet and a $1.5 billion air hub. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon|Gallaugher, Amazon, p.261]]) Running its own shipping saves about a third of what it would pay outside carriers. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon|Gallaugher, Amazon, p.260]]) In the 2020 holidays FedEx and UPS missed a million Christmas deliveries; Amazon still promised overnight. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon|Gallaugher, Amazon, p.260]]) By late 2023 Amazon shipped more than FedEx or UPS. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon|Gallaugher, Amazon, p.259]])
