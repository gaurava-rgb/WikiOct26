# Network Effects

When a product gets more valuable as more people use it. In a marketplace, more buyers attract more sellers and more sellers attract more buyers (a two-sided or indirect network effect). The biggest network tends to pull further ahead, so these markets often end with one or two winners.

### 2025 [[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon|Gallaugher, Information Systems v10 (2025): Ch08 Amazon - An Empire from Cardboard Box to Kindle to Cloud]]

- Gallaugher argues Amazon's data, scale, and network effects across e-commerce, e-books, and cloud have a natural end state of winner-take-all or winner-take-most, giving it huge bargaining power with suppliers and partners. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-923bcb5c7a2b19169482|cite]])
- Opening Amazon to third-party sellers created a two-sided network effect: more customers attract more sellers, which attracts still more customers. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-b5fe25ecdf562e234a62|cite]])
- More than half of shoppers start product searches on Amazon, while only 23 percent of product searches happen on search engines like Google. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-e7b106c6f04153a97a4b|cite]])
- Gallaugher argues third-party sellers that skip Amazon opt out of first-choice consideration for more than half of online product searches, which strengthens Amazon's two-sided network effect. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-41e1b0fbffc6fee1f5fb|cite]])
- Amazon built the Kindle because media made up well over 20 percent of its revenue, and Jeff Bezos saw that media was going digital and that whoever controlled the platform would dominate, since readers want one library on one device. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-ce7ea73c8412103d3f7d|cite]])
- Amazon owned roughly 83 percent of the eBook market. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-e22b5ab1fcf86b2234ec|cite]])
- The Kindle accounts for two-thirds of e-readers sold and fuels over 80 percent of the eBook market, which Gallaugher calls winner-take-most platform dynamics. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-d8399d0648ff9f32a9c1|cite]])
- Alexa became a platform: over 100,000 devices from over 9,600 brands offered Alexa connectivity, so smart home makers must support it or miss the biggest share of the market. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-0f8729a655702604aa2c|cite]])
- Gallaugher argues each Alexa device is a touchpoint gathering behavior data for better AI, but warns that low likelihood of use and added device cost, as with a talking microwave, may thwart network-effect strategies. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-30c2d827f3896b6308ce|cite]])
- Gallaugher argues the Fire Phone failed because it ran an Android fork without most Android apps, so it fought switching costs with a malnourished network effect; its lesson for late movers is that high price, entrenched rivals, switching costs, and missing apps sink a device. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-f3820d68aae2a553bf8b|cite]])
- Fire OS is a modified Android fork, and at launch Fire TV had only 180 apps while the older Roku offered over 1,200. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-89f1d9346ccea98db580|cite]])
- Gallaugher argues AWS's early lead as a platform provider gave it big share, customer lock-in, internal learning, and a network effect from complements such as a growing pool of AWS-skilled developers and software built for the platform. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-abe600c67a2a9979bce0|cite]])
- AWS S3 (Simple Storage Service) works like an infinitely large hard drive; it is one of Amazon's oldest cloud services, so hundreds of software vendors plug into data stored in S3. ([[2025-Gallaugher-Ch08-Amazon#^clm-a7911b11ae02542b0ebf|cite]])
