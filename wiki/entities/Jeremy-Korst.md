# Jeremy Korst

Co-author of the Wharton School and GBK Collective study of corporate AI returns.

## Facts by source

### 2026-10-13 [[2026-10-13-Why-Companies-Cant-Prove-Their-AI-Spending-Pays-Off|Why Companies Can't Prove Their AI Spending Pays Off]]

- A Wharton School and GBK Collective study surveyed 1,400 U.S. employees at firms with at least 1,000 staff and over $50 million in revenue, finding most companies track AI returns by worker efficiency rather than new revenue. ([[2026-10-13-Why-Companies-Cant-Prove-Their-AI-Spending-Pays-Off#^clm-40158a6f5e4fc1b55270|cite]])
- Wharton study author Jeremy Korst said it is very difficult to find materially measurable financial impacts from AI spending, and that measuring returns today rests on managers' perception more than hard numbers. ([[2026-10-13-Why-Companies-Cant-Prove-Their-AI-Spending-Pays-Off#^clm-37cdc14873393fdbd01e|cite]])
- Jeremy Korst argues the current level of AI return is normal for an early rollout phase and in line with past technology adoption cycles. ([[2026-10-13-Why-Companies-Cant-Prove-Their-AI-Spending-Pays-Off#^clm-dc13bc3249a640ba47be|cite]])
- In the 2026 Wharton study 80% of managers reported positive AI returns, and the 28% reporting strongly positive returns were more likely at companies measuring growth outcomes such as new revenue. ([[2026-10-13-Why-Companies-Cant-Prove-Their-AI-Spending-Pays-Off#^clm-e6dbd9f8af2243d3329f|cite]])
- The Wharton study suggests corporate AI spending will keep rising fast only if companies show strong returns tied to growth outcomes like new revenue and output, not cost cutting. ([[2026-10-13-Why-Companies-Cant-Prove-Their-AI-Spending-Pays-Off#^clm-349bd87faa0936d8299b|cite]])
