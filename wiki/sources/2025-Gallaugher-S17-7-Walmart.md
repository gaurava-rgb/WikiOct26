# Gallaugher, Information Systems v10 (2025): S17.7 Walmart - Data Asset in Action and the Rise of Walmart

John Gallaugher, FlatWorld, published 2025.

## Summary

Gallaugher's case on Walmart shows how a physical retailer used data and information systems to reach unmatched scale and low costs. Retail Link turns each checkout scan into reorders, and Walmart shares sales data with over 17,000 suppliers while refusing to share it with data brokers. The section covers Walmart's robot warehouses, data mining, VR training, cashierless Sam's Club Now, and a fast-growing, high-margin ad business. It then tracks Walmart's e-commerce push against Amazon: the 2016 Jet.com purchase, Walmart+, and a $19.5 billion bet on India's Flipkart. It closes with limits: a mature US business, labor and supplier criticism, failed fashion buys, and data that could not foresee the rise of Target.

## Key claims

- Walmart is the largest retailer in the world, and from 2006 through 2009 it ranked either first or second on the Fortune list by revenue. ([p.640] “over the past several years it has popped in and out of the top spot”) ^clm-7e24ca672ce49c7830bb
- Walmart is so big that in three months it sells more than a full year of sales at Home Depot, the number two US brick-and-mortar retailer. ([p.640] “in three months it sells more than a whole year's worth of sales”) ^clm-46ba3cf4bcfe5bb5c1e6
- Gallaugher argues that scale is Walmart's key source of competitive advantage, and that Walmart grew to that scale largely by using information systems more than any retailer before it. ([p.640] “Walmart grew in large part by leveraging information systems to an extent never before seen”) ^clm-aa3290eced59bef41bec
- Gallaugher presents Walmart as the example of a physical product retailer that builds and uses a data asset to get world-class supply chain efficiency aimed mainly at cutting costs. ([p.640] “create and leverage a data asset to achieve world-class supply chain efficiencies”) ^clm-cc14fbcb071e68df7c05
- A McKinsey study found that Walmart accounted for about 12 percent of the productivity gains in the entire US economy at the end of the prior decade. ([p.640] “Walmart was responsible for some 12 percent of the productivity gains”) ^clm-3cff246c1341578f50d8
- Walmart's systems skill is so respected that many senior Walmart IT executives were hired into top roles at Dell, HP, Amazon, and Microsoft. ([p.640] “many senior Walmart IT executives have been snatched up for top roles”) ^clm-b3f12d82cd94499ffc91
- Walmart gathers data on over a million transactions each hour and processes over 2.5 petabytes of data for insights. ([p.640] “gathers data on over a million transactions and crunches over 2.5 petabytes”) ^clm-df98a0c62262c78adbd9
- Walmart claims it has built the world's biggest private cloud, holding over forty petabytes of data and growing. ([p.640] “with over forty petabytes of total data, and growing”) ^clm-861b8309bf9b0a9930a5
- Walmart combines its own data with over 200 outside sources, including weather, economic, Nielsen, telecom, and social media data, gas prices, and local events. ([p.640] “combining its data with over 200 additional sources”) ^clm-355c9533b64d197fdc7a
- Retail Link, Walmart's proprietary and continually refined system, records each sale at the register and automatically triggers inventory reordering, scheduling, and delivery, which keeps shelves stocked with minimal inventory. ([p.641] “not only records the sale, but also automatically triggers inventory reordering, scheduling, and delivery”) ^clm-57d0c29a296e89ef82da
- Walmart rates suppliers on delivery timeliness and schedules truck deliveries to stores less than ten minutes apart to avoid parking lot jams. ([p.641] “deliveries are choreographed to arrive at intervals less than ten minutes apart”) ^clm-caf019fd795b39b45f05
- When Levi's began selling through Walmart, it had to guarantee shelf replenishment every two days; no earlier retailer had asked Levi's for less than a five-day window. ([p.641] “had to guarantee it could replenish shelves every two days”) ^clm-788f5c0187930f7b5b06
- Walmart requires suppliers to ship RFID-tagged goods on a growing list of items, including apparel, jewelry, tires, and batteries, which gives it very accurate inventory, faster reordering, and in-store pickup of online orders. ([p.641] “Requiring suppliers to ship products with RFID tags allows Walmart to keep extremely accurate inventory”) ^clm-f6ff7d3e593c5b76b35b
- Gallaugher argues that Buy Online, Pickup In Store is a Walmart advantage over Amazon, because a pickup trip often leads to extra purchases such as groceries. ([p.641] “is one of the differentiators Walmart has over Amazon”) ^clm-4d9892f09da6a136c16e
- Amazon has hundreds of fulfillment centers, while Walmart has just over thirty e-commerce fulfillment centers but uses RFID to let its stores act as warehouses that ship directly to customers. ([p.641] “RFID allows it to leverage stores to act as warehouses that can ship directly to customers”) ^clm-e88d546d108de99f3257
- Walmart's Scan & Go app lets shoppers scan items with their phones as they shop, keep a shopping list and budget, get app coupons, and pay by showing one barcode at a kiosk. ([p.641] “The firm's Scan & Go app lets customers use their smartphones to scan bar codes”) ^clm-d4449c94776af5a50432
- The book says Walmart spends about $12 million every second on cashier wages across its US stores, so cutting cashiers through self-scanning could save a lot of labor cost. ([p.641] “Walmart spends about $12 million every second on cashier wages”) ^clm-c300055702543fe11edf
- Walmart joined over a dozen retailers, including Best Buy, Target, Sears, and 7-Eleven, to create MCX, a pay-by-phone standard with coupons and loyalty programs. ([p.641] “to plot a new pay-with-your-phone standard called MCX”) ^clm-8f6f3483f001ddf2f63f
- MCX failed despite big-name retail backing, and its technology was eventually bought by JP Morgan Chase. ([p.641] “the effort foundered and the technology was eventually purchased by JP Morgan Chase”) ^clm-21f611fc950b95bcb922
- After MCX failed, Walmart doubled down with its own Walmart Pay app and said it was neck and neck with Apple Pay in acceptance by store shoppers. ([p.641] “the firm doubled down with its own Walmart Pay app”) ^clm-f64ee5030d5bbfbe9805
- Walmart's store-serving warehouses differ from Amazon's: each shipment sends big pallets of hundreds of identical items to store backrooms and shelves. ([p.641] “Walmart's warehouses that supply retail stores are radically different than Amazon's”) ^clm-7e38f5ea68f5c27ed0d2
- Walmart's 1.4 million square foot warehouse in Brooksville, Florida, was its first to use robotics for most products, with robot arms unloading trucks and AI image recognition scanning boxes on five sides. ([p.641] “A 1.4 million square foot warehouse in Brooksville, Florida, was the first”) ^clm-b16e84f788c1840fcaaf
- Walmart's automated warehouses can process more than twice the volume of products of a conventional warehouse. ([p.641] “able to process more than twice the volume of products as a conventional warehouse”) ^clm-bb7088ced5f44dd53c36
- No Walmart workers were laid off due to warehouse automation, but nearly all needed extra training, and some left because they disliked a job with more mental than physical strain. ([p.641] “No workers were laid off as a result of the changes”) ^clm-f8952dec37bcaeb5d734
- Walmart reports lower job turnover in its automated warehouses; the new jobs pay the same as the old ones, but Walmart says they open paths into system administration and robot maintenance. ([p.642] “job turnover is lower in automated warehouses”) ^clm-f84d3c748bbcdc5245e3
- Amazon bought Kiva and builds its own warehouse robots, while Walmart partnered with robotics firm Symbotic and hopes to automate 65 percent of such facilities in the coming years. ([p.642] “Walmart has partnered with robotics firm Symbotic”) ^clm-f2450d0b76e01320c882
- Walmart's data mining showed that Pop-Tarts sales rise sevenfold before hurricanes and beer is the top pre-storm seller, so it sends truckloads of both to Gulf states when a big storm is forecast. ([p.643] “Pop-Tarts sales spike sevenfold before storms hit, and that beer is the top pre-storm seller”) ^clm-7e2b807653b9fd4bdd25
- Walmart uses data mining to tighten operational forecasts, such as how many cashiers each store needs at each time of day, and mined reports drive weekly sales meetings and executive strategy sessions. ([p.643] “predict things like how many cashiers are needed at a given store”) ^clm-1f4b65b9907844ca114d
- Walmart built the Data Café, an analytics center at headquarters on a data trove of over forty petabytes, which sends managers alerts and helps them solve problems with data. ([p.643] “The firm has developed what it calls its Data Café”) ^clm-27b585a7cae9760be146
- Walmart bought social startup Kosmix for $300 million to deepen social media and Big Data skills in @WalmartLabs, which mines Hadoop-stored social posts for insight on products, pricing, and sales leads. ([p.643] “purchased social startup Kosmix for $300 million”) ^clm-a9ef59178785f6ba281d
- Walmart's Global eCommerce unit was a 1,500-person operation based in Silicon Valley across the street from YouTube's offices. ([p.643] “Global eCommerce unit is a 1,500-person operation”) ^clm-4a6cd3adbcdc0c68ba57
- @WalmartLabs grew through acquisitions including OneRiot, Tasty Labs, predictive analytics firm Inkiru, and cloud startup OneOps. ([p.643] “has grown from a slew of acquisitions, including social ad firm OneRiot”) ^clm-bb5344824912c2269f84
- Walmart shares sales data with its suppliers so they can become more efficient, which lets Walmart keep cutting prices, and so they can find patterns that help them sell more. ([p.643] “While Walmart is demanding of its suppliers, it also shares data with them”) ^clm-d7c5a789557c57cfaa73
- P&G's Gillette unit says it mined Walmart data to build promotions that raised sales by as much as 19 percent. ([p.643] “claims to have mined Walmart data to develop promotions that increased sales as much as 19 percent”) ^clm-db78eeb1787b03bc5732
- More than 17,000 Walmart suppliers can see their products' daily sales, shipments, returns, orders, invoices, claims, and forecasts, and they run about 21 million queries a year against Walmart's data warehouses. ([p.643] “suppliers collectively interrogate Walmart data warehouses to the tune of 21 million queries”) ^clm-b043c1e17b39ba9e9472
- Walmart stopped sharing its data with information brokers such as Information Resources and ACNielsen, because broker data added little at its scale and sharing let rivals see its huge share of retail sales. ([p.643] “But Walmart stopped sharing data with these agencies years ago”) ^clm-bacb6f0b8ab506b54eb8
- Walmart custom builds large parts of its information systems to keep competitors off its trail. ([p.643] “custom builds large portions of its information systems to keep competitors off its trail”) ^clm-af0fed0a72e03ebc2243
- Walmart's data center in McDonald County, Missouri, was so secret that the county assessor had to sign a nondisclosure statement before visiting to estimate its value. ([p.644] “the county assessor was required to sign a nondisclosure statement”) ^clm-1825bbde9751e2d61b35
- Walmart has 1.6 million US employees and 2.1 million worldwide, making it the world's largest private employer; only the US Department of Defense and China's People's Liberation Army employ more. ([p.644] “With 1.6 million employees in the United States and 2.1 million employees worldwide”) ^clm-b9a9f0e60ca9999a164c
- Walmart put VR headsets in the back of its 4,600 US stores, and over a million workers have trained on them for tasks like stocking shelves and serving customers; Walmart also uses VR to help pick middle managers. ([p.644] “over a million Walmart workers have used the headsets”) ^clm-56fe86ea9ae0fe887b95
- Walmart's VR training system comes from the startup STRIVR, which also sells it to Chipotle, JetBlue, and Fidelity Investments. ([p.644] “the system provided by the startup STRIVR is now in use at other firms”) ^clm-a39b23b5f2c92765129b
- Walmart answered Amazon Go with Sam's Club Now, a Dallas store with no cash registers or checkout lines, about a quarter the size of a normal Sam's Club with about a quarter of the staff. ([p.645] “Sam's Club Now stores are just a quarter the size of the average Sam's Club stores”) ^clm-1ce8dfae5c898ad928e9
- It took Walmart only five months to develop Sam's Club Now and get it ready to open. ([p.645] “It took the firm only five months for Sam's Club Now to be developed”) ^clm-04f1b56b5b5ff6c3a29a
- The CEO of Sam's Club said most of the technology in Sam's Club Now was built in house, which lets Walmart iterate and move quickly. ([p.645] “That allows us to iterate and move pretty quickly”) ^clm-06cd0067e32ccba3c3a9
- Gallaugher argues that Walmart's response to Amazon Go shows cutting-edge technology can be copied, and that firms with Walmart's scale can build such efforts in house and deploy stores ahead of rivals. ([p.645] “firms with such scale have the resources to pioneer these efforts in-house”) ^clm-7d838cf74f4708a7352e
- Walmart uses robots in stores to check shelf stock, mop floors, and automate deliveries, and claims the robots have cost no jobs and help retention by taking unpopular tasks. ([p.646] “Walmart claims robots have not cost any jobs”) ^clm-6b2b392327e90342794f
- Nearly half of Americans visit a Walmart store, app, or website each week, and that traffic lets Walmart run a multibillion-dollar advertising business. ([p.646] “Each week, nearly half of Americans visit a Walmart store, the firm's app, or its website”) ^clm-d5460e3e461510754880
- Walmart's head of advertising said Walmart can deliver Super Bowl-sized audiences every week. ([p.646] “we can deliver Super Bowl–sized audiences every week”) ^clm-b2ee5510afeff4d10768
- Walmart has over 170,000 ad screens across its 4,600-plus stores and also sells thirty-second ad spots on in-store speakers. ([p.646] “Over 170,000 ad-showing screens are deployed throughout the firm's 4,600+ stores”) ^clm-6467426e33c27e6edf3b
- Walmart's website is a marketplace like Amazon's, so its site and apps offer ads like Amazon's, though at smaller e-commerce scale. ([p.646] “Walmart's website is also a marketplace, like Amazon's”) ^clm-ec5aa3bac04a10a9523c
- Walmart can show advertisers real returns from in-store and online sales and gathers first-party data that firms blocked by cookie and app-tracking limits cannot get. ([p.646] “gather more valuable first-person data that's increasingly unavailable to firms blocked by third-party cookies”) ^clm-789fc155b555659f6a13
- Walmart's ad business earned $2.7 billion in 2022, a fraction of its roughly half-trillion dollars in revenue, but ad margins are typically at or near 80 percent, far above retail. ([p.646] “The ad business was $2.7 billion in 2022”) ^clm-9c1addfa2d999076ba28
- Walmart bought Jet.com in 2016 for $3.3 billion, a young and still unprofitable Amazon competitor, to gain e-commerce expertise. ([p.647] “purchase of Jet.com carried a $3.3 billion price tag”) ^clm-56b7665f8d4694156dd8
- Jet.com's founders had earlier founded Quidsi, the Diapers.com parent that Walmart tried to buy but lost when Amazon outbid it. ([p.647] “which Walmart sought and lost when outbid by Amazon”) ^clm-f772085daff1853be9b6
- In the year after buying Jet.com, Walmart's US e-commerce sales rose 63 percent, and the items sold on its website grew from 10 million to over 67 million. ([p.647] “Walmart U.S. e-commerce sales increased 63 percent year over year”) ^clm-9930ce3618b6b95ffb6f
- After the Jet.com deal Walmart cut its free shipping threshold, gave extra discounts for in-store pickup, and installed pickup towers in some stores. ([p.647] “even installing pickup towers in some locations to streamline this process”) ^clm-825dc8ba7ab432b9ecb1
- Walmart+, Walmart's copy of an Amazon-style subscription, costs about $100 a year and includes free delivery from stores on orders over $35, free shipping, fuel discounts, and Paramount+. ([p.647] “Walmart+ costs about $100 a year”) ^clm-207c09d3bee3b4dd3719
- Walmart+ mobile scan-and-go still requires checking at a self-checkout register, unlike Amazon's just walk out technology. ([p.647] “which, unlike Amazon just walk out, still requires verification at a self-checkout register”) ^clm-543d952f2f151d2ff387
- Walmart has invested a total of $19.5 billion over several years for an 80 percent share of Flipkart, India's largest e-commerce firm; its first stake was the largest foreign direct investment ever made in an Indian company. ([p.647] “invested a total of $19.5 billion over several years to acquire an 80 percent”) ^clm-740fd13c85838e38c8c0
- At the time of Walmart's latest investment, Flipkart had 350 million customers, over 10,000 employees, and sold over 150 million products in 80 categories. ([p.647] “Flipkart had 350 million customers”) ^clm-9c97ca17b8e7fd242d75
- Walmart owns a majority stake in PhonePe, India's largest fintech startup, which has about half of India's mobile payment market and processes about $1 trillion in transactions a year. ([p.647] “PhonePe has about half of India's mobile payment market”) ^clm-674abdafdefc3576e873
- In India almost all of Amazon's investment has gone to AWS, while Walmart leads in dollars invested and in partner share of Indian e-commerce. ([p.647] “almost all of its investments have gone to its AWS cloud business”) ^clm-7e9ba063e6541f2d139c
- Gallaugher argues that Walmart sees India as a market where it can beat Amazon, and that with its US market near saturation it is looking abroad, especially to India, for growth. ([p.648] “Walmart sees India as a market where it can beat Amazon”) ^clm-c020418041b29e940dea
- Walmart bought and later sold several fashion retailers, including Bonobos, Shoe.com, Moosejaw, Bare Necessities, and ModCloth. ([p.647] “The firm bought then sold various fashion retailers, including Bonobos”) ^clm-edcd82f3d54c59fe034e
- Gallaugher argues that Walmart can afford failed small acquisitions as experimental bets, since they barely touch its bottom line and it likely learns and refines strategy with each misstep. ([p.647] “Walmart can afford a portfolio of options that are experimental bets on new businesses”) ^clm-9964d8055fad59569a76
- Walmart grew dramatically in the 1990s, but its US business has largely matured, so it needs huge new markets or dramatic cost savings to raise profits and its stock price. ([p.647] “Walmart needs to find huge markets or dramatic cost savings in order to boost profits”) ^clm-7923d7c3ad623f0d3271
- Walmart faces criticism over low wages, union activism, poor labor conditions at some contract manufacturers, and harm to mom-and-pop stores. ([p.647] “the firm has faced accusations of subpar wages and remains a magnet for union activists”) ^clm-e3f690e79ba096e131cb
- Suppliers face a catch-22 with Walmart: skipping it means missing the largest chunk of world retail sales, but selling to it can force prices so low they cannibalize their own sales at other retailers. ([p.647] “Suppliers that compete for Walmart's business are often faced with a catch-22”) ^clm-fc6c7f8ec85fd2f67def
- In the 1920s and 1930s the A&P grocery chain controlled 80 percent of US grocery sales, with five times as many stores as Walmart has today, before market changes and antipredatory pricing laws brought it down. ([p.648] “the A&P grocery chain controlled 80 percent of U.S. grocery sales”) ^clm-1c39a18fcdbac0ea3b36
- Walmart's data warehouse could not foresee the rise of Target and other up-market discounters. ([p.648] “The firm's data warehouse wasn't able to foretell the rise of Target”) ^clm-17c9bc27bab28eb50b90
- Gallaugher argues that historical data offers little help in adapting to radical changes in retail, so data is a vital tool for managers but not the only one, as Walmart's miss on Target shows. ([p.648] “historical data offers little insight on how to adapt to more radical changes”) ^clm-13482e342484d2624b6d
