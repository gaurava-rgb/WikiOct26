# Meta Settles, A Framework For Regulating Content, The Rest of Big Tech

Ben Thompson, Stratechery, published 2026-08-31.

## Summary

Meta agreed to pay up to $18 billion to settle US state claims that it designed Facebook and Instagram to hook teens, and accepted new guardrails such as time limits and parental consent for safety settings. Thompson says he is torn: defaults help parents, but parenting is a parent's job, and some evidence suggests the teen mental health rise is partly a reporting artifact. He calls the settlement a necessary business decision and finds the product safety argument compelling because it fits his 2019 framework separating what is posted from what the algorithm promotes. He argues the deal may help Meta long term: it can pose as the child safety leader, pressure TikTok, YouTube, and Snap, and collect more data through age checks, which he thinks app stores should handle. He ends by saying it all makes sense and he still hates it.

## Key claims

- Meta agreed to pay up to $18 billion in settlements with US states over social media harm claims, requiring major changes to how Facebook and Instagram operate. (§ Meta Settles: “agreed to pay up to $18 billion in landmark settlements to resolve social media claims”) ^clm-3ca78e8e3d77d3fda4ea
- The settlement requires new guardrails, including limits on how much time youths can scroll and blocking teens from switching off some safety settings without parental consent. (§ Meta Settles: “restricting how much time youths can scroll and preventing them from switching off certain safety settings”) ^clm-3a9ab42aba724ddc2e5e
- The central case was a trial in Oakland where states alleged Meta deliberately designed Facebook and Instagram to encourage compulsive use among young users. (§ Meta Settles: “deliberately designed Facebook and Instagram to encourage compulsive use among young users”) ^clm-9d7f215c75af7b719d7a
- The breakdown: up to $16.7 billion for the Oakland lawsuit, $459 million for other privacy claims, $75 million in legal fees, and a separate deal to pay Texas up to $1 billion. (§ Meta Settles: “Meta has agreed to pay up to $16.7 billion to resolve that lawsuit”) ^clm-8562d64e28db29e67110
- Meta denied the allegations and said the settlement was not an admission of liability; Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers approved it after suspending the trial. (§ Meta Settles: “Meta denied the allegations against it and said the deal did not constitute an admission of liability”) ^clm-13549082ae44062ac3d2
- Thompson argues the politics of social media and children do not split on party lines; the key divide is on the right, between conservatives and libertarians. (§ Meta Settles: “the more important cleavage is on what is usually considered the right, between conservatives and libertarians”) ^clm-8d35104c13ebed68c73c
- Prediction: Thompson argues the settlement sidesteps First Amendment protections and will likely become the blueprint for other cases about policing certain content. (§ Meta Settles: “this settlement will almost certainly be used as the blueprint for others”) ^clm-47cc8fa0721eb2aa1aa0
- Thompson says he is torn: his son's school locks phones in pouches, which he thinks is great, and he sees that defaults set by Meta, such as blocking notifications in school hours, are easier for parents to hold to. (§ Meta Settles: “defaults are powerful and easier to hold to”) ^clm-0c1c5325dc0fa6a935ec
- Thompson also argues limiting kids' phone time is the parent's responsibility, not Mark Zuckerberg's or the government's. (§ Meta Settles: “it’s my responsibility as a parent, not Mark Zuckerberg’s and certainly not the government’s”) ^clm-34530c57a69c6558e383
- The crux of the case is that Meta is blamed for product decisions that harmed children, not for hosting bad posts, which Section 230 would shield. (§ Meta Settles: “because they made product decisions that harmed children”) ^clm-7abfb4a2e6630f012d41
- Thompson cites an NBER paper on New Jersey hospital visits from 2008 to 2019 finding rises in teen suicide-related visits followed screening and coding changes, while self-harm, attempts, and completed suicides stayed essentially flat. (§ Meta Settles: “Rates of other suicidal behaviors including self-harm, attempted suicides, and completed suicides were essentially flat”) ^clm-aa65127aaff1302a6927
- Thompson argues Meta settled as a business decision, and a necessary one: it was losing court cases and faced losing more, with no guarantee of winning on appeal. (§ A Framework For Regulating Content: “Meta was losing court cases, and faced the prospect of losing many more”) ^clm-1b4fd7f6b2800391bd13
- The New York Times reports Section 230 and the First Amendment long shielded Meta, but since 2022 plaintiffs argued the products were designed harmfully, borrowing the Big Tobacco playbook. (§ A Framework For Regulating Content: “taking a page from a strategy used against Big Tobacco in the 1990s”) ^clm-1aa06664cda352ec1110
- Australia became the first country to bar children under 16 from social media, and many other countries have adopted or are weighing similar rules. (§ A Framework For Regulating Content: “Australia last year became the first country to bar children under 16 from using social media”) ^clm-5cc74dcee7259d16b30b
- Thompson finds the product safety argument compelling because it matches his long-held distinction between what users post and what the algorithm promotes. (§ A Framework For Regulating Content: “a distinction worth drawing between what is posted and what is promoted by the algorithm”) ^clm-96bd3d39c8a50cda4f3b
- In his 2019 framework Thompson argued ad-funded platforms deserve their own regulatory category because advertising drives them to maximize engagement, with no payment mechanism to make them care about problematic content. (§ A Framework For Regulating Content: “the incentives of an advertising business drive them to focus on engagement”) ^clm-7f1e42201a31962bf175
- Thompson says COVID radicalized him about the risks of even starting to regulate online content, but he still stands by the overall 2019 framework. (§ A Framework For Regulating Content: “quite radicalizing in terms of the risks of even cracking the door to regulating content online”) ^clm-a968bb8a8186f7584f72
- Thompson would gladly trade Meta accepting teen restrictions for stronger platform free speech protections, but doubts it will play out that way. (§ A Framework For Regulating Content: “I would gladly trade Meta agreeing to restrictions on teens in exchange for a hardening of platform protections”) ^clm-f6c28cfa0abc2d05e319
- A $5.3 billion part of Meta's payout over the next decade depends on rivals committing to similar platform changes and comparable payments; TikTok, YouTube, and Snap have stayed publicly silent. (§ The Rest of Big Tech: “a $5.3 billion portion of Meta’s payout over the next decade depends on whether its industry competitors commit”) ^clm-10d462be62aaf27550a3
- Prediction: Thompson predicts Meta will wear the cloak of child safety, starting with full-screen newspaper ads, to burnish its brand against rivals. (§ The Rest of Big Tech: “Meta is going to wear the cloak of child safety”) ^clm-19b8c28c2ae7717ca41d
- Prediction: Thompson predicts Meta will use the settlement to make TikTok look bad in particular and to downplay YouTube's educational benefits, and would count it a win if TikTok looks like the worst actor. (§ The Rest of Big Tech: “Meta will consider it a win if TikTok ends up looking like the worst actor here”) ^clm-90f0280818eebc3c8089
- Thompson argues Meta is not sad about being required to verify ages, because doing it well means collecting more data, which is great for Meta, now done for the kids. (§ The Rest of Big Tech: “Meta isn’t sad about being mandated to ascertain the age of its users”) ^clm-5bfd9b49bf1b1ac7667f
- Thompson argues that if age verification must happen, app stores should provide it, since the smartphone maker is the highest-trust technology relationship and its operating system already reads everything. (§ The Rest of Big Tech: “if it must be done I think that App Stores providing that verification is the best solution”) ^clm-6f1a16e9d2237f11087b
- Thompson concludes the whole affair feels gross: it pits personal responsibility against bureaucrats dictating how people live, and although everything makes sense, he still kind of hates it. (§ The Rest of Big Tech: “Everything here makes sense, and I still kind of hate it”) ^clm-352bdfb14631f2c17466
