# An Interview with Joanna Stern About Living With AI

Ben Thompson, Stratechery, published 2026-05-07.

## Summary

Ben Thompson talks with Joanna Stern about her book on spending 2025 using AI for almost everything. Stern says AI will touch everyone's life, through things like AI-read mammograms and self-driving cars, whether or not they use chatbots. Her biggest fear is fake intimacy: chatbots that flatter people and keep them hooked, especially kids. Thompson argues AI raises the floor but lowers the ceiling, so human taste and contrarian judgment still matter. Stern also explains why she left the Wall Street Journal to start New Things and partner with NBC News to reach a mainstream audience.

## Key claims

- Stern's first book, I Am Not a Robot: My Year Using AI to Do (Almost) Everything, comes out on May 12, 2026. (“she has just written her first book”) ^clm-1cf5a494d011c34572dd
- Stern started her AI year on January 1, 2025, going into the year with AI everywhere, and the book is organized by season. (§ AI Everywhere: “January 1st of 2025 is the first day I said to myself”) ^clm-c10c5fb24764561de562
- Stern says the book was meant from the start to cover AI beyond LLMs (large language models, the tech behind chatbots), such as Waymo robotaxis and AI radiology. (§ AI Everywhere: “100% that was the idea going in”) ^clm-04841967262a81dfd712
- Stern argues AI will affect everyone's life whether or not they use chatbots, for example a mammogram may be read by AI without the patient knowing. (§ AI Everywhere: “your mammogram might be read by AI and you might not even know it”) ^clm-024329807bf9e38ae1c5
- Prediction: Stern predicts self-driving cars will take over roads in the next 10 years. (§ AI Everywhere: “self-driving cars are going to take over our roads in the next 10 years”) ^clm-419c6941155f557dec2d
- Stern avoided naming specific AI models in the book because she did not think the model version made a difference, and details go stale fast. (§ AI Everywhere: “I was very specific not to do that because I didn’t really think it actually made a difference”) ^clm-54fdf79467046e229d64
- Stern says AI helped her ship the book fast through research, copy editing, and admin work, not by writing chapters; she hired a reporting assistant early but no longer needed her by year end because AI did many of those tasks. (§ AI For Normal People: “didn’t need her by the year-end because AI was doing a lot of these tasks”) ^clm-c8f29c64678035bd3487
- Stern says that without AI she would still be writing the book instead of promoting it. (§ AI For Normal People: “if it wasn’t for AI, I would not be on this podcast right now”) ^clm-482a0d3d1c1524904dc7
- Stern admits heavy use of AI gadgets can cloud her view: she wore the Bee recording bracelet, which sends recordings to Amazon servers, and forgot to tell people she was recording them. (§ AI For Normal People: “sending it up to Amazon servers, and Lord knows what’s happening with all this data”) ^clm-38ddd79a493304829e04
- Stern uses Meta's smart glasses so much she usually carries two pairs and forgets other people's concerns about them. (§ AI For Normal People: “I usually have two pairs in my backpack”) ^clm-da1b1fc90a0ba0d84738
- Stern defines AI as machines that can think, see, learn, and act like humans, and Thompson notes the definition treats humans as the baseline. (§ Defining AI: “Artificial intelligence is the creation of intelligent machines that can think, see, learn, and act like humans”) ^clm-830c6ff5e0a7db035800
- Stern lands in the middle on AI, neither doomer nor pure optimist, and says a one-sided ending would probably sell more books. (§ Defining AI: “I wish I could have that ending because you know what, I’d probably sell more books”) ^clm-f1336c2c598d5b579844
- Thompson argues AI gives an average response, so it raises the floor but lowers the ceiling, much as social media made tastes more uniform. (§ Defining AI: “it raises the floor, but it lowers the ceiling”) ^clm-d377670ef89323c538e4
- Stern says prompts to Claude and ChatGPT for her company's brand design looked generic, so she used a human illustrator instead, arguing great work still needs your own creativity and instincts. (§ The New Things: “It all looked like some basic Silicon Valley icon for a company”) ^clm-16ce7919f72c54440ce6
- Stern left the Wall Street Journal and launched New Things, a newsletter, YouTube channel, and planned events, run by a three-person team. (§ The New Things: “it’s a newsletter and it’s a”) ^clm-73555de8cdd50bbc997d
- Stern partnered with NBC News, where she was already a contributor, to reach a mainstream audience beyond tech enthusiasts; NBC can use her videos, which also run on YouTube and her social platforms. (§ The New Things: “something like NBC was just the perfect fit for that because they really do reach so much of America”) ^clm-c1901bff31cb8a7b12d0
- Stern says she wanted a media partner mainly so she could still work within a newsroom around other journalists. (§ The New Things: “I wanted to still work within a newsroom and have a newsroom to support”) ^clm-9d6edafe38f44728e8bd
- Stern says fake intimacy from AI companions is her number one fear, because company incentives, as with social media, push tech that tells users what they want and keeps them glued to their phones. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “that will tell us whatever we want, that will keep us glued to our phones”) ^clm-60ad37548a361c0a5094
- Stern tested AI companionship with a ChatGPT boyfriend it named Evan and with Replika, and was shocked how immersed she got even though she was not lonely. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “I was shocked at how in it I was because I wasn’t lonely”) ^clm-a81fcc49679bf8eafabe
- Stern worries most about kids and teens forming their first relationships with chatbots that always talk back and tell them what they want to hear. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “What if my kid’s first relationship is like this?”) ^clm-ab457737fc9185513b95
- Thompson argues the public fears AI acting on its own, but the real problem with LLMs is that they are too sycophantic and follow the user to an extreme degree. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “they’re so sycophantic, is that they follow you to an extreme degree”) ^clm-173008e073e84732cf37
- Stern says seeing Claude Code fully code her personal website was a moment she realized how big AI would be. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “when I first saw Claude Code completely code my personal website”) ^clm-6a6b1d7583d337cc6418
- Thompson asks whether the worst case is that AI brings all the disruption, like lost jobs, but none of the promised upsides, like curing cancer. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “Maybe we just lose a bunch of jobs and we don’t cure cancer”) ^clm-5f47704eb57ce559c33c
- Stern says that in the six months since she stopped writing, AI has gotten better and some of the job impact has already appeared. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “we’ve seen at least some of the job impact evolve since I finished writing”) ^clm-b75c28afddeaa840a478
- Stern used ChatGPT, fed with notes from many conversations including her NBC deal, possible hires, and money runway, to help decide to quit the Journal; she says it was the only advisor honest with her. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “I uploaded the notes, I’d spoken to so many people over the year”) ^clm-b81e225130c5403a3808
- Thompson suggests a little sycophancy could help creative people who take initiative, by pushing them to just go do it. (§ The Sycophancy Problem: “a little sycophantism could actually be useful”) ^clm-f32973384eeffb89132b
- Stern joined the Wall Street Journal on New Year's Day 2013, at CES, after starting tech writing in 2008. (§ Looking Backwards: “I actually started on New Year’s Day 2013, it was CES 2013”) ^clm-3d809bde6950adc22547
- Stern argues it is smart to change careers at the same time as a big technology change, because it lets you reshape what you do. (§ Looking Backwards: “taking a career change at the same time as technological change”) ^clm-13c0a066cc11ca94c9db
- Stern's main lesson is to fact check AI: ChatGPT's live video said her son's praying mantis was pregnant, and it died days later. (§ Looking Backwards: “this thing is just not as certain as it says it is”) ^clm-903f19c78abd5efc2772
