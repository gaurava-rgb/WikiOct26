On to the Update:

### The OpenAI Super App

From [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/openai-launches-chatgpt-work-2026-07-09/):

> OpenAI showcased a new AI agent on Thursday meant to help white-collar workers access the power of coding tools without the sticker shock. The agent, called ChatGPT Work, combines OpenAI’s popular chatbot with its AI coding tool, Codex, to create documents, presentations and websites, the company said. The service is powered by OpenAI’s most advanced AI model, GPT-5.6, which also debuted on Thursday. That model’s launch was delayed last month at the U.S. government’s request over national security fears.
> 
> ChatGPT Work is a direct answer to Anthropic’s Claude Cowork, an agent it launched in January that is capable of planning and executing multi-step tasks autonomously. The launch reflects intensifying competition to build and sell AI tools for professional use, as technology companies seek to capitalize on rising demand for autonomous agents that can complete complex tasks with minimal human input. Anthropic and OpenAI — both of which are preparing for possible public offerings soon — are fighting for enterprise business, which is more lucrative than selling products to consumers. Both ChatGPT Work and Claude Cowork are designed for non-coders who want to tap into the abilities of AI coding tools, which are often more capable than standard chatbots, but require specialized knowledge.
> 
> OpenAI officials emphasized that its new offerings would be both cheaper and more broadly available than its rivals’ products. The company launched three different sizes of its 5.6 model.

This was one of the most shocking product Updates I’ve ever experienced — although your experience may have differed! I’m focused in this Update primarily on the Mac app experience, and if you only ever had the Mac app installed, well, you got an updated app that was pretty much the same excellent native app you were used to, but now with access to 5.6 (it’s unclear if it’s Sol, the most advanced model, Terra, the mid-sized model, or Luna, the smallest and fastest model; the model picker only specifies reasoning level).

However, if you had Codex installed — OpenAI’s developer-focused app — then you had a much different experience. Upon updating, Codex renamed itself ChatGPT (with an option to keep the Codex icon), while the old ChatGPT app renamed itself ChatGPT Classic. That appears to be the only supported way to get ChatGPT Classic if you didn’t already have the old ChatGPT installed, because if you download ChatGPT from the OpenAI website it’s the new ChatGPT, which is a rebranded Codex (as the app bundle plainly states).

The shock doesn’t stop there, because ChatGPT — the new Codex-based app — isn’t really a chat app at all, at least at first glance. There is a dropdown menu that lets you choose between ChatGPT Work and Codex…and not chat. There is a chat option in the sidebar, which opens up a floating chat pane on the right side of the window, as if you were on an e-commerce site with an eager LLM looking for a prompt injection. It feels like an afterthought, for an app that has chat in its name.

### ChatGPT = Codex

The reaction to the update has been extremely negative, particularly amongst the old Apple crowd. Gruber wrote on [Daring Fireball](https://daringfireball.net/linked/2026/07/11/evans-chatgpt):

> The old ChatGPT app was focused. That’s the app that still ships for mobile (which includes iPad, which tells you whether OpenAI thinks iPad is a real computer or a big iPhone), and is, for some Mac users, left installed on their systems as “ChatGPT Classic”. The new app is an incredibly confusing sloppy mess. At a glance it looks like a polished app. But the UI is just slop. It has the veneer of a polished app without actually being organized or structured or labeled in ways that add clarity and coherence. It’s playing dress-up as a big-boy app. My understanding from people adjacent to OpenAI is that the company’s senior executives are singularly consumed with FOMO obsession regarding Anthropic, and the only real clout within the company belongs to the AI researchers. Not product designers or app craftspeople. What the researchers say goes, and with this update, we can see their level of taste in app design.

M.G. Siegler wrote on [Spyglass](https://spyglass.org/chatgpt-gets-to-work/):

> It took me a while to figure out what was going on with ‘ChatGPT Work’ – and that was after I watched [the unveiling video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wq45rvPGNHs&ref=spyglass.org). I knew this was a new aspect of ChatGPT, but I assumed the “old” aspect would remain as the default here. You know, chat. Having written a post entitled [“Chat is dead”](https://spyglass.org/chatgpt-is-dead-long-live-chatgpt/) specifically about OpenAI moving away from the chat paradigm for ChatGPT, I also probably should have realized this! But I just didn’t think OpenAI would make this transition so jarring.
> 
> When I see the new ChatGPT app launch into ‘ChatGPT Work’, I instinctively assume it’s an enterprise version of the app. Instead, OpenAI clearly means for ‘ChatGPT Work’ to evoke ‘Claude Cowork’, you know the agentic set of Claude tools, but this is insanely clunky branding. As much as people may know Claude Cowork, probably infinitely more think of Microsoft’s enterprise suite of productivity tools when they see ‘Work’. Or they think of the English language itself. And ‘ChatGPT Work’ makes it sound like it’s ChatGPT to use at work. Like as in your office. As in not for home. Not for personal use.

I’m a bit more sympathetic to OpenAI on one specific vector: Codex, i.e. the new ChatGPT, is one of the most incredible apps I have ever used. [I’ve written about my vibe coding adventures](https://stratechery.com/2026/my-vibe-coding-adventure-the-app-and-the-experience-ten-takeaways/), which have only accelerated, particularly as I have leaned into a model where I run Codex on the Mac Mini in my server rack and access it from anywhere and everywhere, including my work laptop, a MacBook Air I have upstairs, or my phone. This usage model very much feels like a glimpse into the future: the device at hand actually doesn’t matter, as long as it gives me access to my AI.

Another realization I have had is the extent to which Linux and LLMs go hand-in-hand: one of the frustrations of a Mac Mini in the rack is the number of times you have to interact with the GUI thanks to some random warning or permission that macOS will throw up; meanwhile, Docker doesn’t run natively, which means hosting basic apps requires a virtualization layer that is prone to weird failures, and things like SMB connections are flakey. Linux, meanwhile, not only handles all of these things natively and seamlessly, and not only is it designed to be used headlessly via the command line, it’s actually made dramatically more powerful by virtue of being controlled by an LLM, which makes sense: a pure text interface is a perfect match for a large language model, which suddenly gives you dramatically more control and capability than a GUI ever could (Codex is only available on Mac and Windows, but my apps are deployed on Linux).

All that noted, the most surprising experience I had was when I was setting something up with my Synology. Synologys are network-attached storage devices with their own proprietary flavor of Linux that is very GUI-centric; they generally try to steer you away from using the command line interface. To that end, when Codex wanted to make a change, it simply opened up the GUI in its in-app browser, asked me to log in, and started mousing around! It was pretty surreal — and it worked perfectly, albeit much more slowly than a command-line interface.

In short, I can understand the sentiments Stephanie Palazzolo reported on in [The Information early last month](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/inside-openais-decision-combine-codex-chatgpt):

> Nearly 18 months later, OpenAI leaders—including Thibault Sottiaux, who oversaw the new team — came to a stark realization: The company’s Codex coding tool works better for many tasks than its flagship AI service, ChatGPT. For instance, Codex is better than ChatGPT at completing long-running, multistep tasks and at using external tools and writing code to complete tasks such as editing complicated spreadsheets, Sottiaux said in an interview.
> 
> The result is that OpenAI is now casting Codex as a tool that can do much more than coding and that a wide range of people other than developers can use. The company’s release on Tuesday of a research report on how Codex “is becoming a productivity tool for everyone” spotlighted that shift. Codex, OpenAI said, is “helping people across professions automate routine work, move faster and eliminate the bottlenecks of modern knowledge work.”
> 
> Also on Tuesday, OpenAI said that a previously announced combination of ChatGPT, Codex and its browser, Atlas, into a single desktop “superapp” would be launched in the coming weeks. In combining the apps, OpenAI hopes to focus its efforts on its most successful products, OpenAI’s leaders have said, which lately means Codex.

Codex is incredibly capable in a way that ChatGPT simply isn’t. They’re not really in the same category. It’s like comparing the calculator app to Excel: both do math, but the power and possibilities aren’t even remotely in the same ballpark. How many people, however, have any idea what they are missing out on?

### Whither Chat

Just last week [I wrote in passing](https://stratechery.com/2026/muse-image-grok-4-5-alex-karp-on-cnbc/) about the problem with chat in an increasingly agentic world:

> What is worth noting is that this might also be another argument that OpenAI was ultimately harmed by the widespread consumer adoption of ChatGPT, at least absent a compelling advertising business: it’s not just that consumers were a distraction that didn’t subscribe enough even as they used a lot of GPUs, it’s that most consumer AI interactions aren’t particularly useful data for a feedback mechanism. This is in part due to the nature of chatbots — how do you know if the code you generated ran or not? — but also due to the fact that most people aren’t coding. They’re asking about recipes or life or who knows what else, many of which are, if not unanswerable, at least unverifiable.

I guess this sentiment means that while this app launch was shocking, it shouldn’t be surprising? It really does feel, as Siegler noted, that OpenAI is killing chat. Siegler wrote in response to that The Information article, again on [Spyglass](https://spyglass.org/chatgpt-is-dead-long-live-chatgpt/):

> To reiterate, that’s a current OpenAI exec speaking on-the-record about these changes. And his comments also suggest a move beyond the chatbot but also that the company believes we may yet enter a world of “ [one AI to rule them all](https://spyglass.org/consumer-ai-services-apps/) ” – something [I’ve explored more recently](https://spyglass.org/bring-your-own-ai/) in thinking about if the AI world might play out in a similar manner to the old “bring your own device” strategy in enterprise. Is your personal AI going to be so ingrained in your life that it’s also just most convenient to use it as your work AI?

This puts that chat window in the bottom right corner in a different context. Gruber is dismayed — for very understandable reasons! — that the new ChatGPT is abandoning the focus of the old app. That focus, however, behooved an app that was one among many: you opened it up for one specific purpose, and that was to talk to ChatGPT, and you used other apps for other things. This new app, however, is one that is best experienced full screen, with a built-in browser and everything, and a chat window in the corner. It is, for all intents and purposes, a self-contained operating system, but the primitives aren’t your processor and the platform isn’t for apps: the offering is intelligence undergirding an audacious offer to do your work for you.

I do see the vision. ChatGPT, traditionally understood, is threatened in a world of Google shoving Gemini everywhere, Meta manifesting Muse, and Apple launching a Siri that actually works for the things people used a focused chat app for; Codex, meanwhile, is the sort of product that Apple will never launch, Meta will never gain user permission to launch, and Google seems incapable of launching. OpenAI appears to have decided to go all in, trading in their massive head start in terms of a chatbot user base for the opportunity to forcibly introduce AI’s tremendous capabilities to people who probably had no idea.

I’m also not sure if it’s going to work. Codex is incredible and it’s also really complicated and a bit intimidating. It’s also about *work* — being productive — which is something I’m not sure most people are particularly interested in, especially in their free time. ChatGPT v1 may have made OpenAI [the accidental consumer tech company](https://stratechery.com/2023/the-accidental-consumer-tech-company-chatgpt-meta-and-product-market-fit-aggregation-and-apis/); this release seems like a deliberate rejection of that opportunity.

That, though, leaves one very big question mark: if ChatGPT is going to be about work and getting stuff done, then why is OpenAI moving full steam ahead with their advertising product? Advertising is a consumer business model, but this doesn’t feel like a consumer app, at least to the extent you think that consumers are more interested in entertainment and lean-back experiences than they are in agents. Perhaps the answer is that advertising is a mobile story, where the ChatGPT app remains much more of a pure chatbot; maybe the answer is that OpenAI still isn’t quite sure what they want to be.

Regardless, ~~Codex~~ ChatGPT really is an incredible app: using it has made me feel like a kid in the 90s all over again, delightfully discovering technology for the first time, imagining what might be possible, and maybe that’s enough. What is more valuable: 1 billion consumers typing questions and not paying, or a fraction of those engaging ever more deeply such that they can’t help but pay for more? I guess we’re going to find out.
