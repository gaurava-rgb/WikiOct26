# Notes: How OpenAI Beat Math

The Information, Stephanie Palazzolo, 2026-10-08 (AI Agenda newsletter). Deep Dive section by Rocket Drew.

## The core story

- On Tuesday, OpenAI released 722 research papers with AI-created solutions to problems across many areas of math. The author says mathematicians may now feel the anxiety software engineers have felt for months.
- AI can solve ultra-hard math problems even though it still makes mistakes on simple math.

## Why AI is good at hard math

- Math answers can often be checked, much like software code.
- Math overlaps heavily with the work AI researchers do, which they are trying to automate in pursuit of RSI (recursive self-improvement, meaning AI that improves itself).
- With enough computing power, AI can do number crunching that would take humans years, and it does not give up. A mathematician advising OpenAI on how to disclose its math results said the group calls this "brute force and stamina."
- Humans struggle to track dozens of variables at once; models handle many parameters at the same time with relative ease (same mathematician).
- No psychological bias: human mathematicians have overlooked simple solutions because they assumed a hard problem could not have an easy answer. In several cases, OpenAI's models tried those simple solutions and found they worked.
- Humans often give up too fast when hunting for proof that a theory is false, especially a theory that supports their own argument. Models keep looking as long as they get server power.

## Limits

- The advisor says models "miss the forest for the trees." The point of math is often not one hard problem, but using it to build new fields or find general principles.
- If models stay focused on one-off problems, they may never connect ideas into broad discoveries. Humans may keep an edge there, if they do not lean on AI for everything.
- The author notes this is unproven: AI has already shown some ability to connect concepts across fields and suggest solutions or experiments experts miss.

## Deep Dive: AI safety by trial and error

- David Robinson, who led writing of OpenAI's safety reports until his recent resignation, wrote in an essay that OpenAI's culture is broken. His argument: OpenAI improves by trial and error (it calls this "iterative deployment," releasing and fixing as problems appear), which guarantees periodic failures that grow as systems get more capable.
- UC Berkeley professor Stuart Russell (co-author of the leading AI textbook) agreed on the AI Deep Dive show. Asked if trial and error works long term, he said no.
- Russell said it may be the best companies can do on the current path, given a lack of rigorous science for steering AI goals. He said current methods are "not even alchemy."
