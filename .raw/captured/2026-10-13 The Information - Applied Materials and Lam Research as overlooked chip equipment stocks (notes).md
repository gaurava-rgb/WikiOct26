# Notes: Applied And Lam Are Overlooked Chip Equipment Stocks

The Information, Anita Ramaswamy, 2026-10-13 (True Value column, financial analysis).

## The setup

- AI chip stocks look picked clean of bargains: chip designers, chip makers and chipmaking equipment makers have all soared in the past year.
- Applied Materials makes machines for chip etching and deposition, used for memory chips, among the most sought-after parts of AI systems.
- As memory makers expanded manufacturing fast, Applied and its nearest rival Lam Research saw demand for their tools surge.
- Lam and Applied stock have tracked each other closely over the past 12 months and are both up 125% in that time.
- The column argues investors may not appreciate how much growth at both could speed up over the next two years or so.

## Valuation: Applied is cheaper

- Applied trades at a forward price-earnings multiple of 28.8 times, below Lam at 33.7, per S&P Global Market Intelligence. A forward P/E is share price divided by expected earnings for the next year; a lower number means investors pay less per dollar of future profit.
- On a revenue multiple (company value divided by sales), Applied is at 9.1 versus Lam at 11.4.
- The discount makes sense on growth: Lam's revenue grew 24% in fiscal 2025 and slightly faster in fiscal 2026, and is expected to grow 50% over the next 12 months, per S&P data.
- Applied grew only 4% last fiscal year; it is expected to grow 21% in fiscal 2026 and 35% in fiscal 2027.

## The bull case for Applied: DRAM

- Some analysts argue Applied can raise its growth rate to match Lam.
- Applied is stronger than Lam in equipment for making DRAM, the fastest-growing category of memory chip. DRAM is fast, temporary working memory that AI models use to process large volumes of data quickly.
- In their most recent fiscal quarters, DRAM was 14.5% of Lam's total revenue and 20.1% of Applied's.
- About two-thirds of Applied's business is etch and deposition tools, which lay down layers of material on a chip and then carve them away.
- Newer DRAM, high-bandwidth memory (HBM, stacked memory used beside AI chips) and advanced logic chips all stack more layers, so chipmakers need more of those steps.
- Goldman Sachs semiconductor analyst Jim Schneider said Applied has "outsized or overweight exposure to that stuff, which is growing fastest."

## Applied's capacity plan

- Applied has doubled its manufacturing capacity over the last several years, CFO Brice Hill said on an August 2026 earnings call.
- It plans to double the volume of equipment it produces from current levels by 2028, based on "longer-term demand signals" from customers.
- Executives say they decided this by looking at forecasts their large customers give for the next eight quarters.
- That lead time has grown because Applied's customers sign longer-term supply deals with their own customers. The longer view could lower the risk of overproduction.

## Lam: NAND and packaging

- Lam is stronger in NAND, a memory type that works more like a permanent storage vault.
- In the most recent quarter Lam said NAND revenue had doubled compared to the March quarter.
- Industry-wide, NAND revenue is not growing as fast as DRAM. HSBC analysts argued in an August 2026 report that NAND supply could reach parity with demand by the end of 2027, which could slow Lam's revenue growth.
- Lam is also growing fast by selling tools that package memory wafers together onto a chip; executives say this is driving growth in its foundry segment.
- That makes Lam more exposed if AI spending pulls back and hits the memory market.

## Applied's other cushion

- Applied depends more on slower markets like automotive and industrial chips. That hurt its growth the past couple of years but could shield it if AI stumbles.
- In short: Applied has decent exposure to the fastest-growing part of memory, and may suffer less than Lam if AI spending retreats and chipmakers cut equipment orders.

## Risks for both

- China: each company gets around a third of its revenue from China, and both have been hit by trade restrictions limiting what they can sell there.
- Cycles: like memory chip stocks, equipment suppliers are highly cyclical. Years of growth are followed by downturns when chipmakers pull back over oversupply.
- Schneider: "I think both these industries are still cyclical." But he sees "one more year of accelerating growth rate and two more years of growth" at minimum.

## Why it matters for the wiki

- AI shift 2024-2026 story: the AI build-out now flows down to chipmaking tools, with memory (DRAM, HBM) as the bottleneck that pulls the most equipment demand.
- Nvidia journey: Nvidia's demand drives memory makers, which in turn drive Applied and Lam; a pullback in AI spend would ripple down this chain.
- AI-Compute-Demand and Export-Controls: China is a third of revenue for both toolmakers, so US trade limits cut directly into the supply chain's sales.
