### Netflix Earnings

From [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-16/netflix-pledges-more-programs-ai-to-reverse-slowing-growth):

> Netflix said it expects revenue and profit gains to slow in the third quarter, exacerbating investor fears that the company’s growth is topping out. Shares fell more than 8% in after-hours trading, after the company forecast revenue growth of 11.7% — its smallest year-on-year increase of any quarter since late 2023. Netflix executives emphasized on a call with analysts Thursday that the company still has room for growth, noting that it only makes up 5% of TV market share globally…
> 
> The company reported second-quarter revenue of $12.56 billion, up more than 13% from the same period a year earlier. Net income rose almost 9%, to $3.4 billion. The results were roughly in line with analysts’ expectations. Netflix attributed the second-quarter gains in part to recent price increases and growth in advertising.
> 
> Before Thursday’s results were released, the streamer’s stock had already fallen more than 40% over the prior year. The stock decline started when Netflix was exploring a bid for Warner Bros. Discovery’s studio and streaming service—a departure in strategy for the company. It has continued to drop even after Netflix lost out on the acquisition. Netflix said Thursday that viewers watched more than 97 billion hours of content on its service in the first half of 2026, up 1.5% from the second half of 2025 and 1.9% from the first half of last year.
> 
> Netflix executives have been focused on finding ways to get subscribers to spend more time on its service. Such measures are crucial to streamers as they signal that customers are satisfied and less likely to cancel their subscriptions. Netflix’s churn—the rate at which customers cancel—is by far the best in the industry at 2.11% for June, according to research firm Antenna…The company said it will shift to an annual release of its “What We Watched” report, which details how much time viewers spent watching its shows and movies. Until now, it had released that biannually.

Eric Seufert lamented on MobileDevMemo that Netflix is demonstrating [A Troubling Pattern of Opacity](https://mobiledevmemo.com/netflix-q2-2026-earnings-a-troubling-pattern-of-opacity/):

> Netflix made another disclosure in its investor letter that I believe contributes to a troubling pattern of opacity at the company: it will stop publishing its What We Watched engagement report semiannually and instead publish it once per year, in the first quarter. This is the most recent disclosure rollback from Netflix in a steady, systematic practice of withholding metrics:
> 
> - **Forward Subscriber Guidance (Stopped in 2023):** Netflix ceased providing [specific numerical forecasts](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/netflix-stop-reporting-quarterly-subscriber-152000656.html) for upcoming quarterly paid membership additions, beginning in Q1 2023.
> - **Overall Subscriber Numbers (Stopped in 2025):** Netflix stopped reporting total quarterly global subscriber numbers and net additions starting with its Q1 2025 earnings.
> - **Regional ARPU (ARM) (Stopped in 2025):** Alongside total subscriber counts, Netflix permanently withheld its average revenue per member (ARM) by region starting in Q1 2025 to conceal distinct pricing gaps. I had previously relied on these numbers to disprove Netflix’s assertion that its [ad-supported tier would be ARPU neutral](https://mobiledevmemo.com/can-netflixs-ad-tier-be-arpu-neutral/), which it demonstrably [wasn’t](https://mobiledevmemo.com/netflixs-ad-supported-tier-is-arpu-dilutive-enter-proprietary-ad-tech/).
> 
> This is obviously discouraging. As I wrote in [Netflix’s engagement problem is a personalization opportunity](https://mobiledevmemo.com/netflixs-engagement-problem-is-a-personalization-opportunity/), metrics like inter-season engagement decay are not useful, on their own, in assessing the platform’s engagement health. Given the ease of misinterpreting that data, I can understand approaching those data releases with caution. But if Netflix truly believes, fundamentally, that its platform is not mired in an engagement trap and that total view hours are a superficial measure of engagement, it should release more engagement data, not less.

Seufert acknowledged that Netflix co-CEO Greg Peters had a point in his defense that quality of engagement mattered just as much as quantity; from [the earnings call](https://s22.q4cdn.com/959853165/files/doc_financials/2026/q2/Netflix-Inc-_Earnings-Call_2026-07-16T00_00_00_English-1.pdf):

> There is not a linear relationship between view hours and revenue and profit because all hours are not created equal. All hours don’t provide the same kind of value to the business. And a really great example of this is live programming. So live events do a lot of lifting for us for acquisition. They’re good for monetization. They drive ad revenue, fandom. They’re also a promotional platform, but they do not yield typically as many raw view hours. So live, we expect will be 5% of our content budget this year, but we think that will only be 1% of view hours.
> 
> Having said that, 6 out of top 10 new member sign-up days over the past 5 years have come from live events. And if you compare that to another content category, take animation series, kids family TV, it’s also about 5% of our content spend, so the same amount of spend, but it’s going to drive, we expect 8% of view hours. So same spend and 8x the raw view hours. You can see the differences there, even though because as indicated by the amount that we’re investing in both those categories being the same, we think they’re doing the same value for the business.

This is just a portion of a much longer answer that Seufert posted in full, before concluding:

> This is an entirely reasonable position, and it begs the question: why not release those benchmarks in the semiannual engagement report? Even if taken at face value, the ‘competitive advantage’ argument doesn’t explain why the other benchmarks related to quality, variety, and quantity can’t be released. It seems odd for the company to establish the eminently rational and defensible position that total view time is a poor indicator of engagement and then to address that interpretability deficit by simply releasing the same total view time data less often.

It’s a good question! I actually asked Peters about this explicitly [in a Stratechery Interview earlier this year](https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-netflix-ceo-greg-peters-about-engagement-and-warner-bros/):

> **One of the advantages of the member number is it was a very direct line from those numbers to the success of your business and part of that was a function of you had a pretty simple business. You sold subscriptions, and how many subscribers you had was a direct line to your bottom line. Now it’s a bit more complicated. You’ve urged Wall Street to focus on your financial metrics, but of course people want to know what the drivers of those metrics are. To follow on to that point, did you see this sense of a lack of clarity and uncertainty coming? Was that something you anticipated when you thought about changing how you report your earnings?**
> 
> **GP:** Well, the specific subscriber piece was because we were evolving from a model that was really simple. Literally there was like X times Y and you got to the business and as we were doing things like introducing extra members, so you’ve got different membership components. Obviously ARM \[Average Revenue per Membership\] looks very, very different across the world and there’s increasing span in terms of what that looks like in different places. And then the advent of advertising revenue, because now you have this whole other revenue source, which is not directly captured by that math, we knew that that subscriber was going to be less and less complete and trying to drive that out. And so to some degree, the internal way we say this is like, “We’d love to people start to treating us like a business”, and businesses ultimately are about revenue and profit, and that’s the numbers that we should look at, and certainly the numbers that we manage too.
> 
> **Right, I think the problem is people want to get to and know about what the drivers of the business are.**

I’ve by-and-large given Netflix the benefit of the doubt in the case of their reduced disclosures, but when you put them in a list like Seufert did it’s hard to escape the sense that Netflix has a propensity to hide metrics as soon as they are about to turn south on them. And, with regards to Peters’ answer specifically, he cites advertising as an example of how subscriber numbers are not the only driver of Netflix’s business anymore, but advertising is very much a function of engagement, and now the company is reducing the only real engagement numbers it reported.

### Is Netflix Washed?

Andrew Sharp asked on Sharp Text, [Is Netflix Washed Now?](https://sharptext.net/2026/is-netflix-washed-now/)?

> Today’s headline poses a question you’ve probably never thought to ask, so I’ll start with my answer: yes, Netflix is washed now. The content on the platform has never been great, but it’s never been worse. I open the app these days and I’m amazed. What used to be a source of fun, buzzy, compulsively watchable, and occasionally excellent TV and movies is now an endless river of reheated IP, true crime documentaries, and filler dressed as prestige. Millions of people watch this stuff, and everyone instantly forgets it.
> 
> I offer this observation as a swirl of heightened anxiety surrounds the company, so let me clarify one thing up front: I’m not predicting imminent doom. Netflix content reaches a staggering 85% of American viewers and has 325 million subscribers globally. Growth is slowing, but that’s the law of large numbers. If practically everyone in America and much of the world is already subscribed to some version of Netflix, and churn rates are still low, then any concern is relative. Going forward: cable is still dying, and even if the biggest premium distribution platform in the world can’t make great content of its own, it can still license movies, TV and sports rights. Netflix can then spread those costs across hundreds of millions of subscribers and a steadily growing ads business, seeing more engagement in a week than Apple TV sees in a year.
> 
> So no, the company’s not doomed today or destined for collapse tomorrow. Instead, I think what’s interesting to consider is that Netflix has almost certainly peaked. As a cultural force, as a business success story, and as an entertainment death star destined to swallow Hollywood whole, the arrows are all pointing the wrong direction.

I thought Sharp’s piece was clarifying in the context of these earnings, which, reduced disclosure notwithstanding, were pretty solid: all of the numbers were in line with expectations, and while this upcoming quarter’s forecasts were a bit lower than expected, Netflix reaffirmed their full year guidance, suggesting that the fourth quarter will be better. Moreover, all of the company’s visible revenue levers seem to be working: the company says it is still growing its subscriber base, price increases are still being accepted without unexpected churn, and the advertising business is growing. Cash flow was higher than normal thanks to the Warner Bros. breakup fee, but even when you subtract that, the company is still set to generate $10.25 billion in 2026, and that money is going to shareholders: the company bought $4.7 billion worth of shares this quarter.

That’s exactly it, though: Netflix is honestly pretty boring at this point, and, in retrospect, the failed Warner Bros. acquisition feels like an inflection point. Netflix is a surprisingly old company now — its 29th birthday will be next month — but it is a company that has been marked by constant evolution. Co-CEO Ted Sarandos said as much on the earnings call:

> But to take a step back and kind of contextualize this, over the last 15 years, the definition of TV has broadened and our definition has changed along with it. So it’s easy to forget. But if you rewind the clock to, say, 2013, we had a single prestige English language scripted drama show. No unscripted, no local language, no originals, no comedies, no competition shows. And now we’re the #1 creative original programming around the world. Just this week, the Emmy nominations were announced, and we have an Emmy nomination in nearly every category. We didn’t even know back in that first year if House of Cards would qualify for the Emmys. There was a bunch of debate as to whether or not it was TV. So these just announced nominations, I think, are a testament to the quality, the quantity and the variety of our original programming. These expansions are evolutionary, not revolutionary. They are expansions on the same continuum that we started on years ago, adding new things as they become available to us as we see signals that our consumers will get value, including in their Netflix subscription. That continuum has served our members and our business really well. So we’re really excited about the progress.

There are continued examples of that evolution, including the addition of things like podcasts, lifestyle videos from the likes of Condé Nast, and a shift in gaming strategy to the cloud, but all of those are, thanks to Netflix’s size, on the margin. Even the gradual expansion into live events is, as Peters noted, only 1% of time spent on the service. It’s blocking and tackling in the pursuit of methodical growth.

In this view, the attempted Warner Bros. acquisition feels a bit like a last-ditch attempt to escape the inescapable gravity of being big and boring. Yes, Netflix executives spun a fairly convincing story about their ability to monetize Warner Bros.’ back catalog and rich IP library; at the same time, the most surprising thing about the attempted acquisition was how out of character it was. [I assumed it was a response to YouTube’s continued takeover of the TV](https://stratechery.com/2025/netflix-and-the-hollywood-end-game/), but after reading Sharp’s take, I’m tempted to make the comparison to other people in their 20s desperate for one last adventure before they embrace actually being a grown up.

### Additional Notes

Some additional observations from the earnings call:

- Netflix is very much in on live, and will not only continue to pursue global live events, but also regional ones as well.
- Management was asked about potentially partnering with other streamers or potentially acting as a storefront, and did not dismiss it out of hand.
- The company did not close the door on eventually having a free tier, but made clear it wasn’t happening anytime soon, because of cannibalization concerns; at the same time, there was an acknowledgment that a thriving advertising business was a prerequisite.
- The podcast initiative is viewed as a success because it has driven increased consumption during work hours and on mobile.
- It sounds like cloud-based TV games are working well; Seufert noted [on Twitter](https://x.com/eric_seufert/status/2078485685250453836) that the company’s [Game Controller app](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/netflix-game-controller/id6447582581) regularly ranks in the top ten in the App Store. This makes so much more sense for the service than app-based games!

All of these observations, by the way, are of very grown-up things to do. They’re not going to transform the company, but moves at the margins accumulate. All the more reason to embrace being an adult and tell investors exactly what those margins are!
