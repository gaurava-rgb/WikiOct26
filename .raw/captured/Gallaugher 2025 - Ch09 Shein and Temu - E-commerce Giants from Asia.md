9.1 The Overnight Fashion Giant
Learning Objectives
1. Become acquainted with the rapid rise of Shein and Temu.
2. Begin to understand how these firms differ from traditional leading firms in fast fashion and
online retail.
3. Recognize that while these firms have grown quickly, profitability still remains a question, and
firms face multiple challenges.
In about ten years, Chris Xu has created one of the world's greatest sources of wealth. He may
also be Inditex founder Amancio Ortega's worst nightmare. Xu is the CEO-founder of direct-toconsumer fashion site Shein in Nanjing, China (Shein is pronounced “she-in,” a shortened form of
an early, weirder, name SheInside). Now headquartered in Singapore with significant operations in
the Southern China manufacturing hub of Guangzhou, Shein posted $23 billion in sales in 2022,[1]
which ranks as the world's number two fashion firm, just ahead of H&M (2022 sales of $22.25 billion) and behind only Inditex (2022 sales of $35.2 billion). Shein, which has no stores at all and
sells almost nothing within China's borders,[2] claims to be solidly profitable, with sales now spanning 220 countries (the United States, Europe, and Brazil make up the lion’s share of revenue).[3] In
many ways Shein has out-Zara'ed Inditex: drafting a distinctly different form of customer-centered
online shopping experience, using AI and sophisticated analytics to automate the design process,
and leveraging information systems to allow it to unbundle many components of Zara’s verticallyintegrated supply chain in ways that skirt import restrictions and lower costs. Social media is filled
with hashtags like #zaravshein where shoppers post comparisons such as a TikTok with over 10
million views showing a $79 Zara dress from an indistinguishable one purchased through Shein for
$15.[4] While currently known for apparel, Shein has Amazon goals. Its founder audaciously claimed
the firm would have $58.5 billion in sales by 2025.[5] Apparently Xu isn't content to scare Ortega—he
wants a bit of Bezos, too.
The growth of Shein has been jaw-dropping. By 2021, Shein's iPhone app had leapfrogged Amazon’s to become the leading shopping app in over fifty countries, including the United States.[6] At
one point in 2022, Shein raised money at a valuation higher than the market capitalizations of both
Inditex and H&M combined. A “down-round” of financing in 2023 cut the firm's value by ⅓, but privately held Shein raised $2 billion more[7] and was still the third most valuable startup in the world,
behind only TikTok parent ByteDance and SpaceX.[8] That down-round valuing Shein lower than
early expectations comes after many warning flags were raised. Competitors abound. For example,
much of the firm's model has been mimicked by another upstart launched in China, Temu. Temu

Why Study These Firms?
We study Shein, Temu, and related firms for several reasons. First, their breathtaking rise shows
how information systems can be used to rapidly rework an industry's value chain, generate hordes
of new data to be used for product design and predictive manufacturing, create vast amounts of
wealth, shift consumer behavior, and challenge strong incumbents that have created their own
significant assets for competitive advantage. These firms also underscore how lots of e-commerce
innovation is coming from outside the United States. We see a complex interplay between mobile
technology, social media, data collection, AI, and supply chain management. And we get to analyze
these firms, looking to see if they offer true competitive advantage or represent a commodity-style
race to the bottom across suppliers and industries.

Key Takeaways
• Shein has become the world’s number two fashion retailer far quicker than it would take a
traditional brick-and-mortar firm to achieve similar sales.
• Shein uses a model that is different than Inditex’s to get product to market quickly, while
minimizing inventory handling.
• AI is used in product design.
• Shein and Temu hope to challenge Amazon in cross-category e-commerce, but many challenges remain, including reliable profitability (Shein is said to now be profitable, while Temu is
not), fierce competition, legal issues, ethics concerns, and a government climate unfavorable
to Chinese businesses.

The Shein Model
The Shein model pulls in customers, mines data, identifies demands, and brings that demand to
some of the world's most responsive factories. That sounds a lot like Zara, but there are big, important differences that have led to Shein's success.

Shein is able to pull this off in several ways. While Zara was great at taking customer data and
pumping this to designers, Shein goes a step further, using information systems, including artificial
intelligence and data analytics, to mine social media, advertising response, and app usage for
trends. It then uses this in a kind of automated and predictive product design that pushes out manufacturing specs to thousands of small contract manufacturers who use Shein-provided
automation and super-cheap (some have said troublingly cheap)[17] labor to quickly turn inbound
specs into shippable products. Produced lots are tiny—sometimes fewer than 100 goods.[18] Since
Shein has no physical stores, there's really no warehouse stock, and very little unsold inventory
(remember the fashion industry saying “inventory = death”). Like Zara, Shein also lets current
designs run out and replaces them quickly, fueling fashion FOMO and goosing customers to
impulse buy. Once clothes are ready, Shein contract manufacturers then ship directly to consumers
worldwide, and due to a huge current regulatory loophole in the United States and many other
countries, plus some benefits from Chinese regulation, goods skirt taxes, tariffs, and inspections
that large-volume shippers must comply with. This further cleaves costs and vastly improves transit times. Shein and its rivals do advertise heavily—a necessity when there's no store to drop in and
browse on the way home—but the response to new designs shown in ads helps refine styles and
predict manufacturing. And Shein (as well as rivals like Temu) have brought Chinese-style in-app
discovery and shopping “gamification” to hook consumers, keep them browsing, deliver more
design and manufacturing data, and coax them into repeatedly clicking the “buy” button. There are
lots of phrases being used to describe the product-to-manufacture cycle that's so fast. Some call
this “real-time retail.”[19] Others say Shein is more like the meals-on-demand “ghost kitchen” concept where chefs go from food order to home delivery without a physical restaurant.
Shein is often described as secretive, and a lot of what has been published about the firm’s
beginnings may not be accurate. For example, several publications state the firm got its start selling
wedding dresses, but when contacted by reporters at The Guardian, the firm claimed this was
false.[20] A spat between founders is also part of the firm’s early history.[21] We'll set aside the founder
story and take a more useful, in-depth look at various aspects of the Shein model, but we'll also dive
into the many criticisms of the firm, including labor and manufacturing ethics, copyright infringement, environmental concerns, and challenges that could temper the firm's white-hot rise,
including new competitors, legal challenges, and waning customer interest. And we’ll consider
whether or not Shein has durable assets for sustained competitive advantage. Let's suit up!

AI, Mine That Data! Apps, Advertising, and
Social Media
Shein is the largest fashion retailer—online or off—in the United States, and is rapidly growing in
Brazil and Europe.[22] Shein's rapid rise came during the COVID pandemic, a global event that devastated physical retailers worldwide. As an online-only retailer with no physical stores and very little
inventory, Shein was perfectly positioned to grow as global customers opened their smartphones as
their shopping default. And at Shein, what they encountered was a decidedly different experience.

Arm the Influencers!
If you've followed Shein you'll likely know that they've done a bunch of celebrity collaborations,
ranging from work with D-list contestants on The Bachelor[29] to bigger names like Katy Perry and
Khloe Kardashian.[30] Much of this is for marketing purposes; in most cases the celebs aren't really
involved in design. But Shein does lean into an even lower tier of online celebs—social media influencers. While the exact number isn’t known, it’s estimated that the firm has worked with two
thousand influencers in India alone.[31] The company is known for sending free clothes to a large
group of influencers who then post their “Shein Hauls.” The TikTokers, Instagrammers, and YouTubers don't just get free duds; some get a payment and are also given a promo code and earn a
commission based on how many of their followers use it to cash in.[32]
Shein is winning the online word-of-mouth war. The brand is followed by over three times
as many TikTok users as Zara, with fast-fashion laggards like ASOS and H&M trailing even further.[33] That online chatter seems to be paying off. UBS reports that the average Shein customer
spends roughly $100 a month in clothing, 60 percent higher than the average U.S. woman.[34]

Data Is Greater than Vogue
While Zara has done a great job of gathering in-store demand and mimicking catwalk fashions,
Shein doesn't really care about what's on the runway. Instead it responds directly to uncovered
consumer trends by mining social media combined with insights gained from advertising, social
media scraping, and app use. Connie Chan, a partner at venture firm Andreessen Horowitz, sees
this as a new phase of fast fashion where consumers care less about if “Vogue doesn’t think it’s a
cool piece.”[35] Gen Z doesn't read fashion magazines; it's social media that determines what's hot.

The designs that Shein comes up with aren't entirely automated, but Shein's 800-plus person
product team acts as a sort of prototype-designer hybrid operating heavily based on information
compiled and fed to the team by a variety of sources.[36] These teams are less like the traditional
designers you’d find at conventional firms, and more like the next generation of AI worker—assembling solutions based on machine-generated suggestions. Work output by this group is “commissioned” in Shein systems that coordinate with suppliers. Most designs come from Shein, but some
may be selected from a factory’s existing or suggested products.[37] The reduced human touch is
largely responsible for cranking out so many Shein designs, but as we’ll see in a later section,
sometimes AI isn’t the best product designer. Shein has produced several products based on the
unauthorized use of work created by others, and has had several gaffes releasing products that
were racially or culturally insensitive.[38]

Thousands of Partners, Working Quickly,
Coordinated by Software
Once designs are ready to be manufactured, proprietary Shein software is used to immediately commission that product for manufacture by what the firm determines is the perfect manufacturing partner. Shein deliberately chooses thousands of small to mid-sized contract manufacturers capable of quickly producing smaller product runs. Shein has convinced its supplier-partners to
ditch their own software in favor of a Shein custom-built information system, which tightly coordinates Shein’s entire supply chain network. One analysis compares the Shein model of contract
manufacturing to Uber, but for contract manufacturers instead of contract drivers. Anyone meeting Shein's requirements and maintaining its standards can play.[39]

That first commission for manufacture is almost always a small order, perhaps just a few dozen
products.[40] Surfacing the new product in ads and through the app thus becomes a sort of A/B
test.[41] If data-driven evidence shows they've got a hot product on their hands, Shein will order a
larger run, perhaps involving multiple suppliers quickly cranking out the same design. Thousands
of items are being tested simultaneously. Shein refers to this model—real-time data, to trial test, to
manufacturing more units if warranted—as the large-scale automated test and reorder (LATR)
model.[42] Sometimes Shein's systems and prototypers come up with products that no one wants,
but the cycle occurs so quickly and with so few products in the first run, that losses from misfires
are minuscule—a low single digit when compared to the 30 percent write-off levels of conventional
retailers.[43] Another thing that sets Shein suppliers apart—in most cases suppliers ship individual
items directly to consumers' homes, not as a large bundle of manufactured products shipped to
a retailer's own warehouses. This isn’t quite just-in-time manufacturing but it’s close, and with
each order part of either a small limited-run test or a larger order based on a data-driven prediction
of product demand, suppliers never carry a large finished-product inventory for long. It's estimated
that at any given time, the hundreds of thousands of products you can find in the Shein app have
been live for three months or less (for comparison, at Zara the number of products less than three
months old is about 53 percent.[44] By refreshing thousands of products daily, Shein has conditioned
customers to make a habit of visiting the app—fail to be a regular and once again, be prepared to
experience fashion FOMO.

of units to produce. The system also provides a regular assessment of which products failed, and
which are selling well and are worthy of a larger unit run. Shein also provides software coordinating direct-to-consumer shipping, and tracks information through to customer product receipt,

Shein Grew by Taking Advantage of Import
Loopholes
One reason that U.S. customers receive Shein’s mostly China-sourced orders so inexpensively, and
so quickly, is due to a quirk in U.S. customs known as the provision of the Trade Facilitation and
Trade Enforcement Act.[51] At the time of this writing, items imported into the United States are only
subject to duty when the value of the shipment is over $800. So unless a Shein supplier ships over
$800 worth of goods in a single order, the order arrives with no import duty assessed. Also, China
hasn’t taxed exports by Chinese direct-to-consumer companies since 2018.[52] As one industry pundit
puts it, “If you’re Zara, there’s no way you’re going to get around U.S. import duties because you’re
not shipping to individuals. You’re selling to stores and importing in bulk.”[53]
Also key, the U.S. Tariff Act of 1930 states that orders of $800 or less also avoid custom checks
that larger shipments are subject to. This accelerates direct shipments to consumers since goods
aren't waiting in line for port inspection.[54] Shein goods almost always arrive in less than two weeks,
and many in just four days.[55] Not as good as Amazon speed, but many consumers are more than
willing to be patient as long as they shell out significantly less for a new wardrobe.
Shein's traditional competitors have noticed that the firm’s goods have been sliding to consumers through greased loopholes, and they aren't happy. As will be pointed out later, lawmakers
in the United States (as well as in other nations with similar provisions) are exploring legislation to
close the factory-to-consumer de minimis provision, so it's uncertain how long Shein will be able to
milk this to their advantage. The lighter inspection regime for these orders has also raised concern
among many U.S. lawmakers, since inspectors aren’t performing the kinds of checks that would
allow them to track whether these low-cost shipments violate import restrictions, such as a ban on
products produced from cotton sourced from China’s Xinjiang province[56] (Shein has denied it uses
Xinjiang cotton, although that province is the source of about 87 percent of the nation’s cotton output).[57] Shein is building warehouses in the United States, in part likely in anticipation that it will
have to tweak its current strategy once laws change.[58]

Key Takeaways
• Shein gathers data from a variety of sources, including the firm’s app, social media monitoring, advertising response, and via purchase from third parties.
• This data, including AI image recognition technology, is used to compile insights used by
Shein designers, allowing them to create data-driven products based on what the firm
believes will be desirable trends. Shein designers represent a new category of AI knowledge
worker, less a traditional fashion designer and more an assembler based on compiled and
distilled input from the information system. The breadth of Shein’s data gathering and AI
operation allows the firm to create thousands of new products a year, many more than even
Zara.
• Also unlike Inditex, Shein doesn’t own any of its own factories. Instead it works with thousands of contract manufacturers who adopt Shein information systems to supply designs,
coordinate production, provide shipping information, and track customer receipt and perception of product quality.
• The supplier system is used to assess supplier performance, with Shein able to cut suppliers
if the firm feels it has not met the firm’s standards. Shein can also provide firms with suggestions for future improvement.
• The system allows Shein to go from the start of product design to a manufactured order at
a speed faster than Zara—only three days for some products. Suppliers also manufacture in
very small lots, allowing Shein to lower risks from poorly received goods, order more supply
and greater variants of well-performing products.
• Shein advertises heavily, in part because the firm has no physical store presence and needs
to remain top-of-mind. It also works very closely with social media influencers to fuel awareness and spur demand.
• The Shein app is a different experience, focusing on browsing-style product discovery rather
than the search style emphasized by most e-commerce sites like Amazon. The Shein app
has greater average use time despite having fewer product categories than Amazon, allowing Shein to gather even more usable data for product creation, manufacturing runs, and
customer profiling.

Tone cited a pattern of “loose oversight and poor working conditions” among several of Shein's
suppliers. It also found that some Shein manufacturers would subcontract orders to smaller, lowercost suppliers with no direct Shein oversight.[65] An undercover operation performed by a UK news

firm has zero tolerance for forced labor and has a robust compliance system, including a code of
conduct, independent audits, robust tracing technology, and third-party testing.[69]
Shein can only expect more scrutiny as its growth attracts the attention of advocacy groups
and concerned legislators. And if Shein goes public it will have to be more open. As mentioned
earlier, a loophole in U.S. shipping law allows Shein to ship goods directly from supplier factories
to consumers with much less oversight than traditional firms that import large supplier orders.
Critics have claimed, among other things, that this has allowed Shein to import goods made with
cotton from China's Xinjiang region. Imports from this region are banned under U.S. law, citing
widespread human rights abuses against the region's Uyghur (pronounced “wee gur”) minority.
Bloomberg News had tested Shein products and found goods containing the banned cotton.[70] After
a U.S. congressional hearing, Shein strongly denied claims of widespread Xinjiang cotton, claiming
its own extensive tests conducted by third parties found nearly 98 percent of cotton did not come
from the banned region, and that when cases using Xinjiang cotton were identified, “production
of affected products was stopped and any products containing cotton associated with the positive
tests were removed from sale.”[71] Many remain dubious of Shein's defense, especially given the prior
noted fact that roughly 87 percent of Chinese cotton comes from Xinjiang. Another set of tests conducted on apparel collected by U.S. Customs and Border Protection showed that roughly 27 percent
of goods contained cotton from Xinjiang.[72]

PR Backlash: A Big Thumbs Down from Shein’s Influencer Trip
In an apparent attempt to counteract criticism of how it produces products, Shein invited several
influencers on an all-expenses-paid trip to visit partner factories where it showcased open and

testimonies, praising what they learned about the firm during their visit. One influencer with four
million TikTok followers posted: “Upon interviewing the workers, a lot of them were really confused and taken back with the child labor questions . . . They weren’t even sweating. We were the
ones sweating!” Another influencer who described herself as an “investigative journalist” gushed
at “how excited and impressed [we were] to see the working conditions.”[73]
However, most know that influencers are not investigative journalists, and the social media

company?” and “the gaslighting is CRAZY!”[75] Others questioned why Shein chose to spend the

AI-fueled profiling, Shein still, it seems, has a lot to learn about communicating with integrity.

Dangerous Products
Knock-offs are bad, but outright dangerous products are even worse. Shein has faced several troubling product recalls in recent years. These include infant pajamas that were deemed a burn hazard
and in violation of U.S. flammability standards,[88] and a girl's toddler trench coat and bag that, when
tested by Health Canada, had toxic lead limits twenty times greater than the amount allowed under
Canadian law.[89]
Shein certainly isn't the only firm with product recalls, and some mistakes seem inevitable
when so many products are produced. But testable chemical violations are more likely to be discovered in firms that are subject to more thorough oversight imposed on large-volume importers.

testing, the potential number of dangerous products in circulation could be significantly larger
than recalls suggest. Unlike Shein’s pledge to improve its information systems and design oversight
in the IP violations above, this issue is more process-focused, and will require Shein to more vigilantly and proactively assess supplier production. If not, the result of flaws could be far more
significant thank a stolen logo.

and properly dispose of goods sold. Players beyond fashion should begin planning for more
stringent environmental laws and increasing responsibility for the the impact and disposal of
their products.

Going Big, Not Going Home
Shein's headquarters move from China to Singapore is one way it has signaled its global ambitions.
Opening another international headquarters in Ireland, long a hotspot for global operations of tech
titans, as well as a low-tax haven for stashing global profits, is another. The less reliance Shein has
on China, the more nimble the firm will be in shifting sources to suit the firm's economic and political goals. Brazil, already a $100 million consumer market for the firm,[98] was also home to over one

to better address the cultural preferences of given countries.

Marketplace Strategy: Less Like Zara, More
Like Amazon
The biggest shift in Shein's future plans is the launch of the firm's “marketplace strategy.” Like
Amazon and competitor Temu, this will allow third-party firms to list their own brands on the
Shein site. In the platform-focused marketplace strategy, a firm never takes possession of goods,
nor does it book ownership of the inventory. While this means less revenue per item, it usually
means far greater profits. Amazon has found the strategy so successful that the firm now has
over 60 percent of its business coming from marketplace resellers rather than from goods that
Amazon buys from wholesalers and that it would book as part of its own inventory.[101] If the plan
works, there are lots of potential benefits for the firm. More products could attract more consumers
to spend even more time on the site, and result in more data- and AI-driven buying insights. An

A Dark or Shiny Bright Future for Shein?
Currently only about one-third of all fast fashion sales happen online. This might suggest that
Shein (as well as Temu and competitors) has lots of potential upside.[123] These firms have created
data-fed, AI-assisted platforms that bring some of the world's lowest cost and most nimble clothing
suppliers to a global market. But in addition to the concerns outlined in the prior section, Shein
is also experiencing slower growth. In the first half of 2023, Shein's revenue growth slowed to just
13 percent, compared to nearly 60 percent growth in 2022 and a 223 percent spike in 2021. The reason for the slowdown is unclear. Increased competition could play a role, customers’ souring on
ultra-cheap fashion could be another. Customers enticed by low costs may also be having second
thoughts on the value of what they've purchased. One reporter reviewing the service cited dissatisfaction with the quality and fit of most of the order she'd placed.[124]
Finally, as a technology and business strategist, one wonders what in the Shein model is a true
source of sustainable competitive advantage. Shein is open to competition. Temu shows the model
is replicable. Shein's attempts to lock-up suppliers is a way to gain assets and potentially scale, but
it's up to the courts to decide if this is legal. Data, refined over time and more usable in AI systems
that competitors can't match, would be one benefit, but competitors like Amazon and Alibaba also
have a ton of data and deep expertise in AI and customer-driven analytics. Can Shein build a brand
before existing firms fight back or new competitors arise? What all managers should take away
from the Shein example is how quickly technology can be used to weave together a globe-spanning
web of value in new, low-cost, customer-serving ways. These threats will be a constant throughout
your career. Stay focused, informed, and always current on industry and technology developments.

Key Takeaways
• Shein has begun to expand sales to new markets and has grown its supplier base outside of

has also moved its headquarters abroad and is rapidly expanding globally.
