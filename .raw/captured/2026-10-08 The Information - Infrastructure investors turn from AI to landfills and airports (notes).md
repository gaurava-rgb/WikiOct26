# Notes: AI’s Boom Has Infrastructure Investors Looking at Garbage

The Information Finance newsletter, Alex Eichenstein, 2026-10-08.

## The core story

- For a few years AI drew investors away from dull sectors like transportation and waste, as billions went into data centers.
- Now investors are keen on boring assets that produce steady cash, such as landfills and rail terminals.
- Investors argue that much smaller but essential assets, like regional airports, can give attractive returns.

## Partners Group's view

- Nicholas Pepper, a managing director in the infrastructure business of Partners Group, says the heavy focus on digital infrastructure leaves high-quality mature assets in utilities, transport and social infrastructure getting less attention.
- Pepper: this is "creating opportunities to acquire these assets at attractive valuations."
- Partners Group is an asset manager with $186 billion in AUM at midyear 2026. AUM means assets under management: the total money it invests for clients.
- Partners Group closed its fourth infrastructure fund at over $15 billion in July 2026.
- It has also piled into data center infrastructure. Its deals include an initial $1 billion investment in AVK Power Solutions, a power provider for data centers in Europe, announced in August 2026.

## How much money AI has pulled in

- The capital needed for the AI buildout has pulled record sums into infrastructure investing.
- Blackstone has made AI a major theme across its infrastructure, credit, private equity and real estate businesses.
- In October 2025 a group of investors including BlackRock's Global Infrastructure Partners bought Aligned Data Centers at an enterprise value of around $40 billion, the largest such sale at the time. Enterprise value is the price of the whole company, including its debt.

## Signs investors are getting pickier on AI

- There are growing signs that investors in AI-related assets have become more selective. The causes: construction delays, rising financing costs, and regulatory pushback that could slow the AI buildout or hurt its payoff.
- These show up in delayed IPOs (first public stock sales) by data center developers.
- They could also stall the predictable cash flows that make infrastructure attractive.
- Traditional infrastructure has not suffered much from the AI focus. That is unlike software, which fell out of favor because investors feared AI would hit those businesses directly.

## Where the money is going instead

- Investors name transportation and logistics, aviation, waste and water, bridge and road maintenance, and gas processing. These usually bring stable cash yields and contracted or regulated revenue (income fixed by long contracts or set by regulators).
- Middle-market investors (those doing mid-sized deals) say they are well placed because the biggest firms moved toward AI, leaving less competition for small deals.
- Brett Stevenson, founder and managing partner at Canadian private equity and infrastructure firm BTG Capital, says big U.S. investors' minimum check sizes are now so large that they skip this segment, giving BTG "a huge economic moat." A moat is a lasting edge that keeps rivals out.
- BTG has sold several power facilities to data centers, but most of its investing is in transportation, logistics, energy and utility assets that are not data center plays.
- In June 2026 BTG bought the then-closed Stephenville International Airport in Newfoundland, planning to restore and modernize it. It reopened on Oct. 2, 2026.
- Stevenson calls airports a very scarce asset class, and says scarcity can drive multiple arbitrage later. That means buying at a low price relative to profits and selling at a higher one.

## Waste management

- Morgan Stanley Infrastructure Partners, which has made several data center investments, is focusing on waste management, per a person familiar with the matter.
- The draw: long-term municipal contracts, low exposure to commodity prices and geopolitical tension, and growth in regions trying to rely less on landfills.
- U.S. waste companies trade at about 15 times Ebitda, versus 10 times for European ones, per the same person. Ebitda is a rough measure of operating profit (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), so a higher multiple means a pricier company.
- U.S. firms command that premium because they control more of the chain, from garbage pickup to treatment plants to the landfills.

## Transportation looks cheap

- Freight and U.S. trucking, hit by post-pandemic overcapacity and higher fuel costs, are cyclical bets that could be due for an upswing.
- Geopolitical tension that disrupts shipping routes has pushed valuations down across transportation and logistics.
- That may be a good entry point for infrastructure investors, who often compete with strategic buyers (companies in the same industry) for ports and rail terminals.
- The median Ebitda multiple for strategic deals in transportation and logistics fell to 5 times in the first quarter of 2026, from 12.7 times in 2025, per middle-market investment bank R.L. Hulett.

## Why it matters for the wiki

- AI shift 2024-2026 story: the AI buildout is now big enough to reshape whole investing markets, pulling capital away from older infrastructure.
- Another sign of AI investors growing selective (delayed data center IPOs, rising financing costs), which fits the debt-fueled data center story.
- The software selloff is named as a direct AI fear, which ties to the SaaS pressure story (Workday and others).
