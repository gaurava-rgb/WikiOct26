# Notes: SpaceX Shakes Up Data Center Leadership After Aggressive Build-Out

The Information, Grace Kay, 2026-09-01 (exclusive).

## The core story

- In recent weeks Elon Musk replaced several leaders on the SpaceX team that builds its data centers. The new leaders come from SpaceX's rocket and satellite internet businesses (per people familiar with the matter).
- The reshuffle follows civil engineering concerns and steady reliability problems at SpaceXAI's existing data centers in Tennessee and Mississippi (per two people).
- In its early rush, SpaceXAI ran those facilities for months without backup cooling and backup power in place (per four people). That raised the risk of outages.
- The shakeup adds doubt about the timing of new sites. SpaceXAI has pushed back, several times, its plan to start building outside its Memphis, Tennessee hub, including in Texas (per two people).

## Musk's power warning

- Speaking virtually at a G20-related gathering, Musk warned that a "crisis of power" will arrive by next year, of at least 15 gigawatts. A gigawatt is a unit of electric power; 15 GW is roughly the output of 15 large power plants.
- His point: that shortfall would leave a large number of AI chips in data centers unable to switch on. He urged other countries to build more power.

## Who came in

- Wesley Salandro, former SpaceX vice president of production for the Falcon and Dragon rockets (per one person).
- Logan McConnell, director of product support operations at the Starbase launch site (per one person).
- An engineer from Neuralink (Musk's brain implant company) to help with civil engineering.
- Job postings show SpaceX hiring for several civil engineering roles.

## Who left

- Jake Palmer, who led physical infrastructure for SpaceXAI, left in late July. He had joined in September 2025 from data center operator Hut 8.
- Also gone: Zach Wells, who managed the data center build-out, and Pablo Mendoza (per the people and LinkedIn data).
- Mendoza had been head of finance early on, reporting to Jared Birchall (described as Musk's right-hand man), then took a director role on the infrastructure team (LinkedIn and a 2025 org chart).
- Earlier exits over the past year: Brent Mayo, manager of site builds who coordinated much of the work with the city of Memphis, left for OpenAI. Liz Balke, who came from Tesla's Dojo supercomputer project, now works on Anthropic's data center infrastructure team (LinkedIn).

## The business backdrop

- SpaceX pitched fast data center construction as a key edge ahead of its large IPO in June. An IPO, or initial public offering, is when a company first sells shares on the stock market.
- It had about 1.4 GW of compute capacity online as of June and expects more than 2 GW by year end. Musk says capacity will rise sharply by end of 2027.
- SpaceX signed major compute rental deals with outside firms including Anthropic and Google. Compute rental means renting out its data center chips to other companies.
- Those rentals drove AI revenue to $2.6 billion in Q2 2026, up from $737 million a year earlier.
- Musk has said the rental deals are flexible and SpaceX can take capacity back later if needed.
- In August SpaceX closed its $60 billion all-stock purchase of coding startup Cursor, meant to boost sales of its own AI products. "All-stock" means it paid with its own shares, not cash.

## Why it matters for the wiki

- Compute scarcity beat: even a builder that brags about speed hit reliability limits (no backup cooling or power), and Musk now names power, not chips, as the next bottleneck.
- Model labs vs clouds beat: SpaceX acts as a landlord, renting compute to Anthropic and Google, while keeping the right to pull it back for its own AI.
- Talent flow: data center builders move between SpaceXAI, OpenAI and Anthropic, showing how scarce this skill set is.
